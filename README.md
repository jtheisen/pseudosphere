# Pseudosphere

A React / TypeScript / Vite viewer of an analytic pseudosphere, with an intrinsic hyperbolic tessellation rendered in a fragment shader.

```sh
corepack pnpm install
corepack pnpm dev
```

Build with `corepack pnpm build`. If pnpm is already installed, omit `corepack`.

`pnpm test:geometry` checks the shader's intersection mathematics on the CPU, including 6,600 rays aimed at known surface points across steep camera angles, plus axial and horizontal cases. It also models float32 arithmetic for rays near the central axis and horizontal direction, guarding against cancellation in the turning-point offsets. Fish checks cover kite side rotations, folding handedness, neighbor colors, head-to-tail hypercycles and atlas coverage without holes or overlaps. These checks do not launch a browser or verify the actual GPU driver.

Drag horizontally to turn and vertically to tilt, using a mouse or touch. Scroll to zoom. Releasing a moving drag applies inertia: horizontal rotation decays at 3.5/s, while tilt decays much faster at 18/s. Grabbing again stops momentum; a pause of up to 300 ms before releasing preserves momentum, while a longer pause does not fling. Tilt is clamped to approximately ±89°. Resetting the view or changing the tilt slider stops momentum. Rotation is accumulated without reducing modulo 2π; the coordinate branch cut stays at the back.

The viewer shows GPU render time using `EXT_disjoint_timer_query_webgl2`, with a rolling mean and 95th percentile over the latest 120 valid samples. The main percentage is mean GPU render time divided by the median observed animation-frame interval over up to 120 frames. The display includes both times and observed frame cadence, adapting to different refresh rates. This is a portion of the observed frame interval, not overall GPU utilization; browser throttling or sustained dropped frames can lengthen that interval. Timing encloses the render pass and excludes React/UI work and display refresh waiting. Queries are read asynchronously; GPU-disjoint results are discarded. Changing any view/pattern setting or rendering resolution resets the samples and skips 30 warmup frames. Dragging, inertia, and automatic rotation suspend benchmarking. Read the metric at a stationary view after the sample count reaches 120. Browsers without the timer extension show “Unavailable”; CPU frame timing is not substituted.

The only rasterized geometry is a full-screen quad. Rays intersect the unbounded analytic surface r = sech(t), y = t − tanh(t). The solver evaluates the height gap y − profile(r), whose stationary points along a ray reduce to a quadratic in squared radius. Splitting at those points creates at most three monotonic sections; the first sign-changing section is refined with up to 32 bisections. This handles overlapping projections without marching past paired intersections or exhausting a marching budget. Each ray's entry and exit from the infinite radius-one cylinder bound its search, with only the natural wide rim at y = 0. Axial rays are handled analytically. There is no upper height cutoff or surface fade. Camera tilt spans approximately −89° to +89°; near −89° the upward vanishing point is in view. Pixel resolution and floating-point precision limit the narrowest visible detail, and unresolved texture detail is averaged.

For u = θ and v = cosh(t) = 1/r, the induced metric is (du² + dv²)/v². Mapping the upper half-plane into the Poincaré disk and reflecting through a triangle with angles π/p, π/2, π/q produces the regular {p,q} tessellation. For the procedural ornaments, choose {4,5} squares, {3,7} triangles or {4,6} squares. The separate “Pure geometry · {4,6}” option fixes the latter tessellation: its six 60° corners at each vertex make opposite edges continue along the same geodesic. The {4,5} edges are also geodesic, but its five-way vertices have no opposite edge. Triangles have 5/14 the intrinsic area of the {4,5} squares. The pattern-position control applies an upper-half-plane dilation, an isometry; congruent tiles keep their intrinsic size. The unmatched circumferential pattern joins on the hidden back side.

Circle Limit fish is the vector option. It uses a separate (4,3,3) triangle rotation group: four right fins, three left fins and three noses/tails meet. `src/fish.ts` authors an original vector fish whose boundary curves are paired by exact hyperbolic rotations. Nearby copies form a small reusable SVG atlas, rasterized once by the browser and uploaded as a mipmapped texture. The fragment shader folds surface coordinates into a kite and samples this local engraving; it never samples a bitmap of the complete Poincaré disk. A four-color permutation representation keeps edge neighbors different and head-to-tail chains the same color. White backbones follow the exact hypercycle through the kite's tail and nose, rather than geodesics. Explicit texture gradients avoid mipmap streaks at folding boundaries. The square and triangle ornaments remain available; their tessellation selector is independent of the fish's fixed geometry.

The fish are drawn from above, with paired oval eyes, a tapered body, swept fins and curved engraved fin rays. Body widths follow the available silhouette around the narrow tail. Each fish's engraving is clipped to its outline, and both atlas panels are clipped independently to prevent offscreen ink from corrupting the color labels. The geometry follows Douglas Dunham's [A “Circle Limit III” Calculation](https://www.d.umn.edu/~ddunham/dunbrid07.pdf) and [More “Circle Limit III” Patterns](https://www.d.umn.edu/~ddunham/dunbrid06.pdf). The motif is an original interpretation, not a scan or exact tracing of the woodcut.

“Fish from the print” is a separate selectable bitmap experiment. It uses a local crop of the original [Circle Limit III image](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Circle_Limit_III), registered to the same kite by a disk isometry. The crop includes the fish pieces needed at kite boundaries, preserving original engraving and eyes; pigment hues are permuted as the pattern repeats. The source JPEG is kept unchanged in `public/textures`, and cropping happens through shader texture coordinates. Only the central patch is sampled, so detail does not deteriorate as rotation explores further regions. Its roughly 64-pixel fish detail limits enlarged resolution; small boundary mismatches remain possible because the woodcut and image registration are approximate. The vector option remains available for comparison.

“Fish from the plane” is now the default experiment. The supplied Drawing 123 image remains unchanged at 1280 × 944; no upscaling or generated detail is applied. `src/planarFish.ts` registers a central repeating kite around the yellow fish, approximately 200 × 339 original pixels. The shader converts its hyperbolic half-kite into Klein coordinates, maps it to the planar half-kite, and smoothly adjusts the lateral coordinate so the straight source backbone becomes the exact hypercycle. The other half shares the same fin edge and uses the corresponding source reflection. The deformation changes the planar (3,3,3) pattern to hyperbolic (4,3,3), rather than wrapping a flat pattern around the surface. Source pigment fragments receive their new neighboring-fish labels before color permutation. Jacobian-based texture gradients prevent fold seams from selecting overly blurry mip levels. The source's hand-drawn and photographed registration imperfections may still appear at some joins; the native full-disk preview checks the mapping, while actual browser/GPU rendering remains for interactive verification.

The fold solver uses a finite reflection budget, and the cusp uses pixel-footprint filtering. Extremely large accumulated rotations can eventually lose precision in GPU floats. WebGL is required. Fonts load from Google Fonts, with local fallbacks.
